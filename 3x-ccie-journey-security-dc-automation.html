3× CCIE Journey — Lessons from Security, DC & Automation Tracks

Three CCIEs taught me three different ways of thinking. Security taught paranoia, Data Center taught systems, Automation taught leverage. The certificates expire; the thinking doesn't.

Why three tracks

I didn't set out to collect numbers. Each track followed where my work actually was: securing government infrastructure pushed me to CCIE Security, an N7K-to-ACI Multi-Pod migration demanded CCIE Data Center depth, and the realization that I couldn't scale myself led to CCIE Automation. The lab number is a milestone; the months of structured study are the product.

What each track really teaches

  • Security: think in failure modes. The lab forces you to ask "what happens when this control is bypassed?" for every design. That habit — assuming compromise, designing the second line — is the entire job, and it's why the track pairs naturally with CISSP-level strategy work later.
  • Data Center: think in systems. ACI, VXLAN/EVPN, storage, compute integration — nothing in the DC track is a single-box problem. You learn that fabric behavior emerges from the interaction of overlays, underlays, and policy, and you stop debugging boxes and start debugging systems.
  • Automation: think in leverage. Python, APIs, model-driven telemetry, pyATS. The mental shift is from "how do I configure this" to "how do I make this class of change safe to repeat a thousand times." It changed my career more than the other two combined.

The study method that survived three labs

  • Lab time beats reading time 3:1. Build everything. Break everything. My EVE-NG topologies were rebuilt from scratch weekly until muscle memory took over.
  • Teach to learn. Writing explanations — for colleagues, for myself — exposed every gap I'd been papering over. (This blog is the continuation of that habit.)
  • Timed mock labs from month three. The exam is a time-management test wearing a technology costume. Train the clock skill explicitly.
  • Protect the routine. Two focused hours daily beat weekend heroics. Across years and a demanding job, consistency was the only variable that mattered.

Was it worth it?

In an era of AI assistance and cloud abstraction, people ask if expert-level certs still matter. My answer: the credential opens doors, but the capacity for sustained deep work the journey builds is what walks through them. Every architecture review, every P1 bridge call, every "can we trust this AI-generated config?" decision draws on judgment those labs forged.

If you're starting: pick the track your current job feeds, not the one that's trendy. Studying what you practice daily halves the journey.
