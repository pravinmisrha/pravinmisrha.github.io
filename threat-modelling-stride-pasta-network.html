Threat Modelling with STRIDE & PASTA for Network Engineers

Security reviews that start from the firewall rulebase find firewall problems. Threat modelling starts from the attacker's goals — and finds the paths your rulebase never imagined.

Why network engineers should threat model

We're trained to think in flows and policies. Attackers think in goals and paths. Threat modelling is the discipline that bridges the two: enumerate what can go wrong before the design ships, while changing it is still cheap. Having reviewed application code for XSS, CSRF, and SQL injection in a previous role, I can say the same systematic mindset transfers directly to network architecture — the assets just become VRFs, control planes, and management interfaces.

STRIDE: the fast structural pass

Walk every component and trust boundary of the design and ask six questions — Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege. Applied to a typical enterprise design:

  • Spoofing: Can a rogue device impersonate a RADIUS server or a BGP peer? (Answers: server certs in ISE, BGP TTL security + auth, DHCP snooping/DAI at access.)
  • Tampering: Is config integrity protected end-to-end — signed images, RPKI for routes, change pipelines instead of ad-hoc CLI?
  • Information disclosure: Where does cleartext management traffic still live? SNMPv2, syslog over UDP, that one legacy TACACS path everyone forgot.
  • DoS: Control-plane policing, CoPP on every box, and capacity assumptions written down — not discovered during the attack.
  • Elevation: Who can pivot from a user VLAN to the management VRF? In most environments I assess, the honest answer is "more people than intended."

PASTA: when the stakes justify depth

PASTA (Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis) is the seven-stage, risk-centric method: define business objectives, decompose the application/infrastructure, enumerate threats, map attack paths, and weigh likelihood against business impact. Where STRIDE gives you per-component coverage, PASTA forces prioritization — it answers "which of these forty findings do we fix this quarter?" That's the language a CISO and a budget owner actually share, and it aligns cleanly with NIST CSF and ISO 27001 risk-treatment requirements.

Making it stick

Make threat modelling a design-gate artifact: no new network segment, exposure, or third-party connection goes live without a one-page STRIDE table and a list of accepted risks with named owners. Thirty minutes per design. It will find something every single time.
